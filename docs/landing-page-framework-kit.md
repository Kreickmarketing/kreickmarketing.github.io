# SaaS Landing Page Framework Kit

**For the AI reading this:** you are rebuilding a landing page for a **SaaS product** (software people sign up for and pay for monthly or yearly). Use the messaging framework and page format below.

How this kit is organized:

- **Part 1, the messaging framework:** what to say, level by level.
- **Part 2, the FATE test:** how to check every headline.
- **Part 3, a worked example:** ClearMark, a real business, done well.
- **Part 4, SaaS adjustments:** how the framework changes for software.
- **Part 5, the page layout:** section by section, with SaaS sections.
- **Part 6, conversion rules:** the details that decide whether visitors sign up.
- **Part 7, rules:** writing rules.
- **Part 8, inputs to collect:** what to ask the owner for.
- **Part 9, your task and the output format:** what to produce.

ClearMark sells a service, not software. Use its example only to see the *shape* and *quality bar* of each level. Do not copy its wording, numbers or claims. Where software needs something different, Part 4 overrides the example.

## Part 1: The messaging framework

Write it top to bottom. Each level comes from the one above it, and the proof at the bottom holds everything up. If a level can't be backed by a real proof point, rewrite it until it can.

### A. The promise (what every channel ultimately says)

| Level | What it is | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | Names the reader's problem in their own terms, as a sharp, slightly provocative statement. | 1 sentence, under 10 words |
| **Tagline** | The action or outcome you deliver, as a short command. Doubles as the sign-off. | 2 to 4 words |
| **Company promise vs. hook** | The *company promise* is the broad line behind everything you sell (use it in bios and anything spanning offers). The *hook* is the sharper line for one audience (use it as the headline on everything aimed at them). | 1 line each |
| **Brand promise** | The concrete change in the customer's life or business, in plain words. No adjectives about yourself. | 1 sentence |
| **Sign-off** | Brand name + tagline. Ends every piece of content. | 1 line |

### B. The foundation (who it's for, what you are, why it works)

| Level | What it is | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Value proposition** | What you build, why it works, and one hard number that proves it. | 3 sentences |
| **Positioning statement** | Who it's for (be specific: role, company size, situation) → what you are → how you work → why the alternatives fall short → what they get and how fast. | 4 to 5 sentences |
| **Target audience** | The exact buyer, the moment they feel the pain, who they trust, and what they need to say yes (usually proof + a low-risk start). Note secondary and parked audiences separately. | 2 to 3 sentences |
| **Offer ladder** | Every way to buy, from cheapest entry point to biggest commitment. For each: name, price range, who buys it, and status (ready or draft). Say which one the page is selling. | 1 line per offer |

### C. The message (the elevator pitch)

**Primary message:** headline + tagline, then the problem in one sentence, what you do in one sentence, the best proof in one sentence. Close with how working together goes (timeline, cadence, how success is measured). 2 short paragraphs.

### D. What sets you apart

**Three selling points.** Each one has:
1. A short title (4 to 7 words, concrete, ideally with a number).
2. Benefit sentence in the form *"[Customer] gets [result] because [your mechanism]."*
3. One real example sentence.
4. A proof line: 2 to 3 hard facts (numbers, scale, time).

### E. The pillars

**Three content pillars:** themes every piece of content comes back to. Each pillar is a benefit statement with **three proof points** underneath (specific, numeric where possible).

### F. Supporting proof

- **One quote** from a credible third party (name, title, company). Only use it with their approval.
- **How the team works:** who the customer deals with day to day and what you promise about communication. Only promise what you will always do.

## Part 2: The FATE test (check every hook and headline)

Before a headline or hook is final, it must pass all four:

- **F: Focus.** Does it stop the scroll and hold attention?
- **A: Authority.** Does it show you know this world from the inside?
- **T: Tribe.** Does the right reader think "that's people like me"?
- **E: Emotion.** Does it make them feel something (relief, worry, pride)?

If it fails one, rewrite it. Give 3 to 5 options and say which letter each is strongest and weakest on.

## Part 3: Worked example (ClearMark)

ClearMark builds AI production systems for creative agencies. This is its finished framework. Study the shape; don't reuse the content.

**Headline and tagline**
> Your single source of truth shouldn't be a person.
> Make it visible.

**Company promise vs. hook**
> Company promise: "Making your workflows work." Hook (agency audience, FATE-tested): "Your single source of truth shouldn't be a person / Make it visible."

**Brand promise**
> Your agency keeps delivering when your best creative is out of the room, and your clients can see the work well enough to buy more of it.

**Sign-off**
> ClearMark. Make it visible.

**Value proposition**
> ClearMark builds artificial intelligence (AI) production systems your agency's team can see and run. They work because the system comes first, and a creative director with 26 years inside agencies builds it. For PayPal in 2024, putting 300+ creatives on one shared journey board led to a new email strategy that lifted click-through from 17% to 54%.

**Positioning statement**
> ClearMark is an AI creative systems practice for owners and production leaders at agencies of 15 to 200 people, led by a creative director who has spent 26 years inside agencies. We build the production system first, a shared view of the work running in the tools you already use, then add AI and train your team to run it. AI consultants have often never run production, and tools leave the setup to you. We deliver a working prototype in three weeks and a live cycle to prove it.

**Target audience**
> Owners, managing directors, and heads of production at agencies of 15 to 200 people, where one person knows where every job stands, or where clients have been promised AI and automation that isn't fully built yet. They trust practitioners over consultants and want proof in agency terms and a low-risk way to start.

**Primary message**
> Your single source of truth shouldn't be a person. Make it visible.
> When every job runs through your best creative, the agency stalls when they do. We build AI production systems your team can run, and a shared view of the work that makes your next idea easier to sell. For PayPal, putting 300+ creatives on one board led to a new email strategy that lifted click-through from 17% to 54%.
> We build one pilot at a time: a working prototype in three weeks, one live cycle to prove it, and then your team runs it. You'll hear from us twice a week while we build, and the live cycle ends with a measured result.

**Selling points**
1. **One board your client can see.** Account teams sell more ideas because agency and client review the whole journey in one view. *Proof: 300+ creatives on one journey board; email click-through from 17% to 54%.*
2. **The AI you sold, actually built.** Agencies deliver on their automation promises because we build the system before the client notices it's missing. *Proof: 500+ ads rebuilt into one live system in under three months.*
3. **Two people, 480 assets, 49 hours.** Agencies get volume without adding headcount because a custom design system handles the repeat work. *Proof: 480 posts and reels in 49 working hours; a design system of 120+ images.*

**Pillars**
1. **Everyone sees the work, so ideas sell faster.** Proof: 300+ creatives on one board; click-through 17% → 54%; client approved changes in real time in one shared file.
2. **Your agency runs on a system, not one person.** Proof: 300 tracked tasks replaced by 63 visible outcomes, cutting launches from 360 days to 16 weeks; a new team member trained to run it in two days.
3. **AI production that delivers what you promised.** Proof: 500+ ads rebuilt in under three months; 480 assets in 49 hours.

## Part 4: SaaS adjustments to the framework

Software is bought differently from a service. The buyer usually tries it before talking to anyone, compares you with 2 or 3 alternatives in the same browser session, and worries about switching cost, security and price. Adjust the framework like this:

| Framework level | Change for SaaS |
|---|---|
| **Headline** | Name the outcome or the painful job the software removes, not the category. "Close your books in 2 days, not 10" beats "Accounting automation software." |
| **Tagline** | Can be the product's core action ("Ship it visible.") or the outcome. Keep it 2 to 4 words. |
| **Value proposition** | Add *what it replaces* (spreadsheets, a manual process, a competitor) and *time to value* (how fast a new user sees the first result, e.g. "set up in 10 minutes"). |
| **Positioning statement** | Name the category you compete in, and the 1 to 2 alternatives the buyer is really comparing (including "doing nothing" or "a spreadsheet"). Say what you do that they don't. |
| **Target audience** | Separate the **user** (who uses it daily) from the **buyer** (who approves the spend). Name the trigger moment that sends them searching. |
| **Offer ladder → pricing tiers** | Becomes Free / trial → Starter → Pro → Business or Enterprise. Per tier: price per month (and the annual discount), who it's for, the 3 to 5 features that differ, and the limit that makes people upgrade (seats, projects, usage). Mark one tier "Most popular". |
| **Selling points → key features** | Still three, but each is a **feature written as a benefit**, shown with a product screenshot or short clip. Format: *"[Feature name]: [what the user can now do] so [business result]."* |
| **Pillars** | Use for blog, SEO and email topics, not on the page itself. |
| **Proof** | Software proof is: customer count, named logos, usage numbers ("2M invoices sent"), before-and-after metrics, review scores (G2, Capterra, App Store) and security certifications (SOC 2, GDPR). |
| **How the team works → support and onboarding** | Who helps new customers (chat, onboarding call, docs), response times, and migration help. |

## Part 5: SaaS landing page format

Build the page in this order. Each section pulls from a framework level, so the page and the framework always agree. Sections marked *optional* can be cut on a short page.

| # | Section | What goes in it | Comes from |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Navigation bar** | Logo, 3 to 5 links (Product, Pricing, Customers, Docs), "Log in", and the main button. | — |
| 2 | **Hero** | Headline, 1 to 2 sentence subheadline (value proposition, shortened), main button + secondary button (e.g. "Start free" + "Watch 2-min demo"), a short reassurance line under the buttons ("No credit card. Cancel any time."), and a **product screenshot or short looping video** of the real app. | Headline, value proposition |
| 3 | **Logo strip** | "Trusted by 1,200+ teams at" + 5 to 8 customer logos. | Proof |
| 4 | **Problem** | Short heading + 2 to 3 lines naming the pain and the current bad workaround (spreadsheets, manual work, a clunky tool). | Primary message, paragraph 1 |
| 5 | **How it works** | 3 steps with icons or small screenshots: Connect / Set up → Use → Get the result. Mention time to value. | Value proposition |
| 6 | **Key features** | The three selling points. Each one alternates image and text left/right: feature title, benefit line, 2 to 3 bullets, screenshot. | Selling points |
| 7 | **Results / metrics** | 3 to 4 big numbers with short labels ("43% faster reporting", "4.8/5 on G2"). | Proof |
| 8 | **Testimonials** | 2 to 3 quotes with photo, name, title, company. Best: a quote that includes a number. *Optional:* one short case study card. | Supporting quote |
| 9 | **Integrations** *(optional)* | "Works with the tools you already use" + logo grid. | Positioning |
| 10 | **Pricing** | Tier cards (Part 4), monthly/annual toggle, "Most popular" badge, one button per tier, and a "Compare all features" link. Enterprise tier says "Talk to sales". | Pricing tiers |
| 11 | **Security and trust** *(optional for consumers, required for business buyers)* | Certifications, data hosting, uptime, privacy in plain words. | Proof |
| 12 | **FAQ** | 5 to 8 questions that answer real objections: price, contract length, switching from a competitor, data import, security, support, what happens after the trial. | Objections |
| 13 | **Final call to action** | Repeat the headline idea in new words + the main button + the reassurance line. | Headline, tagline |
| 14 | **Footer** | Sign-off, product links, company links, contact, privacy and terms, social links. | Sign-off |

## Part 6: Conversion rules for SaaS

These are the details that decide whether a visitor signs up.

- **One primary action, repeated.** Pick one: *Start free trial*, *Get started free* or *Book a demo*. Use it in the nav, hero, after features, in pricing and at the end. Self-serve products under about $100/month lean to "Start free"; complex or expensive products lean to "Book a demo".
- **Button text says what happens.** "Start my free trial", not "Submit" or "Learn more".
- **Remove sign-up worry next to every main button.** For example "No credit card required", "Free for 14 days" or "Cancel any time". Only say what's true.
- **Show the real product early.** A real screenshot in the hero beats an illustration. Label key parts of the screenshot if it's busy.
- **Above the fold** (what's visible before scrolling, on a laptop and a phone): headline, subheadline, main button, product visual. Nothing else is essential there.
- **Be specific.** Numbers, names and timeframes beat vague claims every time. "Saves 6 hours a week" beats "Saves time".
- **Answer objections before they're asked.** Price, switching effort, security and "will my team actually use it" belong in features, pricing or FAQ.
- **Pricing is clear.** Show prices if at all possible. Hidden pricing loses self-serve buyers.
- **Mobile first.** Short paragraphs, buttons easy to tap, screenshots that are readable on a phone (crop to the key part).
- **Search basics.** Give a page title (under 60 characters), a meta description (under 155 characters) and one H1 that matches the headline.

## Part 7: Writing rules

- **Plain language.** Short sentences. Explain any jargon in a few words. No hype words ("revolutionary", "cutting-edge", "synergy", "seamless", "all-in-one").
- **Proof beats adjectives.** Every claim needs a number, a name or a timeline. If there's no proof, cut the claim or mark it *[needs proof]*.
- **Never invent** numbers, customers, logos, quotes, reviews, certifications or results. Ask for them, or leave a clearly marked placeholder.
- **One audience per page.** The headline and features speak to one buyer.
- **Customer as the hero.** Sentences start with what the customer gets, not what the company is.
- **Benefits first, features second.** Lead with the result, then name the feature that delivers it.
- **Unnamed customers stay unnamed.** If proof is under an NDA, describe the customer by type ("a 40-person accounting firm").

## Part 8: Inputs to collect before writing

Ask the owner for anything missing from this list. Don't start writing until you have the items in bold.

1. **Product name, and what it does in one sentence.**
2. **Who uses it, who pays for it, and company size or type.**
3. **The main problem it solves, and what people use today instead.**
4. **Top 3 features and the result each one gives.**
5. **Pricing:** tiers, monthly and annual prices, trial length, and whether a credit card is needed.
6. **Primary action:** free trial, free plan or demo.
7. **Real proof:** customer count, logos you're allowed to show, metrics, quotes (with permission), review scores.
8. Main competitors, and why customers choose you over them.
9. Integrations.
10. Security and compliance facts.
11. Top 5 to 8 questions or objections prospects raise.
12. Brand voice (3 adjectives), plus any words to avoid.
13. Available visuals: screenshots, demo video, product GIFs.

---

## Part 9: Your task and output format

1. **Collect inputs.** Ask for anything missing from Part 8. Don't move on until you have the bolded items.
2. **Write the framework.** Fill in Part 1, level by level, with the SaaS changes from Part 4, in the same format as the example in Part 3.
3. **FATE-test the headline.** Give 3 to 5 headline + subheadline options, score each against F, A, T and E, and recommend one.
4. **Write the page.** Write the full landing page copy section by section, following Part 5. For each section give:
   - the section number and name
   - all copy: headings, body, bullets, button labels, small print
   - a one-line **visual note** (what screenshot, image or layout goes there)
5. **Add search basics.** Give a page title, a meta description and alt text for each image.
6. **List the gaps.** End with everything marked *[needs proof]* or *[placeholder]*, so the owner knows what's still missing.
